§ 01 — How We Got Here

No parent asked for this. A tech company did.

The 1:1 Chromebook model didn't emerge from parents demanding it or from teachers asking for it. It emerged from a specific corporate strategy, adopted quickly, and never seriously audited.

In 2002, Maine became the first state to issue laptops to every schoolchild. The rollout was slow and the evidence mixed. Then, in the early 2010s, Google began pitching a low-cost alternative — a stripped-down laptop running its free cloud software, with its email and productivity tools built in. Sales staff reached out to district administrators. Teachers were asked to promote the product. By 2017, Chromebooks made up more than half of all digital devices shipped to U.S. schools; by 2026, according to The New York Times, 88 percent of American public schools have adopted some form of 1:1 policy, issuing a personal device to every student. Apple, Microsoft, and Google had been competing for a decade not to build the best educational tool, but to capture the largest possible share of American children as lifelong customers of their ecosystems.

Duluth adopted the 1:1 take-home model for middle and high schoolers in that context, alongside nearly every other district in Minnesota, and rolled Chromebook carts into its elementary classrooms at the same time. It was presented as modernization, and teachers and administrators made those choices in good faith with the information they had at the time. The research on whether the 1:1 model actually helped kids learn came later — and it has not matched the original promise.

Now the reckoning is underway. On March 29, 2026, The New York Times published Natasha Singer's "Chromebook Remorse" investigation, documenting how schools from Kansas to Michigan to North Carolina are pulling devices out of classrooms after discovering students were watching YouTube, playing games, and bullying classmates on their school-issued accounts. The Times followed it with a major opinion piece on April 19. At least sixteen states — including Minnesota — have introduced 2026 legislation to restrict school screen time, require efficacy evidence for edtech, or allow parents to opt out. Kansas, Utah, Missouri, and others are moving toward K–5 device bans entirely.

The kids it hurts most are the ones least able to push back.

Any parent of a neurodivergent child knows this already: a Chromebook is not a neutral object in the hands of a kid with ADHD, autism, a sensory processing disorder, or an anxiety diagnosis. It is a dysregulation engine. The constant pull of notifications, the endless tabs, the video autoplay, the games one click away — these are not small frictions for a brain that is already working twice as hard to attend to a teacher in a classroom. They are the single largest source of lost instructional time that a district can create for its most vulnerable students, and then hand them in a cart every morning.

The accessibility argument is often used to defend 1:1 programs: text-to-speech for dyslexic readers, speech-to-text for students with writing difficulties, closed captions for hard-of-hearing students. Those tools matter. They belong in the IEPs and 504 plans of the students who need them, delivered on the devices those students need. That is different, though, from putting an internet-connected laptop in front of every child in every classroom and counting it as inclusion. According to CHADD — the national ADHD advocacy organization — roughly seventy percent of people with ADHD go on to develop depression, and researchers have repeatedly documented that neurodivergent children are more susceptible to compulsive screen use than their peers. A 1:1 program designed without these facts clearly in view is not accessibility. It's something else, and our students deserve better thinking.

A speech pathologist's view

Denise Champney has worked with neurodivergent children in Rhode Island public schools for twenty-five years. She watches students "complete" iReady math assignments without reading the problems: "They are just clicking; they want to get through it."

What she sees in her students with ADHD and autism, she explains, is not deeper learning but a shallower version of it — kids who get very good at guessing which button to press, at picking up surface patterns in a test interface, while the harder work those same students most need — sustained reading, writing a sentence from scratch, building something with their hands — gets quietly pushed off the schedule. And the laptop itself, she observes, has become a social-escape hatch: when a peer interaction gets hard, the device provides a frictionless way to disappear into a video game. Multiply that across a school day, every school day, and the result is a learning environment that is falling short for the very students it was meant to help.

Source: The New York Times, Opinion, "A Greatest Blunder in the Past Decade of K-12 Education," April 19, 2026.

What Take Tech Out is asking for is not the elimination of assistive technology for the students who genuinely need it. It is the elimination of the 1:1 default — the assumption that every child should carry a Chromebook home every night — and the return of a model where technology is a specific tool for a specific lesson, delivered when it demonstrably helps.

§ 02 — The Problem

Duluth put a Chromebook in every child's hands. Then what?

Duluth Public Schools calls it "Digital Innovation." The district's 1:1 take-home Chromebook program begins in middle school and runs through 12th grade.

The district frames the program as teaching "digital citizenship" and preparing students "for the workplace." These are reasonable goals. But as the national evidence has accumulated, it has grown harder to argue that the 1:1 model — piling hours of screen time on top of the hours our kids already spend on screens at home — is the right vehicle for meeting them. A 2021 study of 11,875 American children ages nine and ten found that higher screen time was associated with worse mental health, more behavioral problems, lower academic performance, and poorer sleep. A 2024 cross-sectional analysis of school-aged children found the same pattern for sleep quality and academic performance.

In the United States, The New York Times has reported that math and reading scores among 13-year-olds peaked in 2012 — the year before Chromebooks hit the 50-percent mark in American schools — and have been declining since.

The issue isn't only the screens. It's also the software now running on them. Kahoot, the quiz-game company, reports eight million teacher users worldwide. iReady, a gamified math and reading platform, is used by roughly seventeen million American children — about one in three students from pre-K through twelfth grade. These apps are designed, by their own product teams, to hold children's attention the way social-media platforms hold adults' — through streaks, points, badges, cartoon avatars, and confetti animations. There is a growing body of evidence that what looks like engagement in those interfaces isn't always learning.

§ 03 — The Money

$4.2 million is being cut from Duluth classrooms. Edtech spending is not.

On March 24, 2026, Duluth Public Schools announced it had approved $4.2 million in cuts for the 2026–27 school year to close a $4 million shortfall. Staff "displacement" — the district's term for moving or eliminating positions — begins in April. Middle and high schools lose $1.78 million. Elementary schools lose $1.26 million. The classroom is bearing the bulk of the reduction.

At the same time, the 1:1 Chromebook program in middle and high school, the classroom-cart devices in elementary, the Digital Innovation department, the software licenses, and the device replacement cycles all continue as budgeted. We understand the district has difficult trade-offs to make, and that administrators and the School Board have not taken these cuts lightly. But we think the balance between the two categories — classroom staffing and educational technology — deserves a fresh public conversation before the reductions take effect.

Sources: Duluth Public Schools, "Budget Reductions for 2026–2027 School Year" (March 24, 2026); ISD 709 Digital Innovation department page. A data request under the Minnesota Government Data Practices Act has been filed with ISD 709 Business Services for actual line-item technology expenditures in FY24 and FY25.

§ 04 — The Research

What the data actually says about screens in school.

Below are seven pieces of evidence we think every Duluth parent, teacher, and school board member should read. Click through. Decide for yourself.

UMD · Expert Alert

"Screen Time and Kids" — Prof. Aaron Boyson

The UMD Department of Communication's expert alert lays out the basics: children now consume eight to ten hours of screen media per day on average, not counting schoolwork. The American Academy of Pediatrics' original recommendation of zero screen time for the youngest children has been quietly walked back, Boyson notes, in ways not obviously driven by research. A short, clear read.

Read at news.d.umn.edu →
NYT · Opinion

"The Greatest Blunder" — The April 19 NYT opinion piece

A synthesis of the current evidence against universal 1:1: gamified apps that train unusually narrow skills which do not transfer once the screen is off (the point neuroscientist Jared Cooney Horvath makes throughout), speech pathologists watching neurodivergent students click through iReady without reading, principals describing the social wreckage of online classroom tournaments. 88% of American public schools now follow a 1:1 policy. The piece argues — and Take Tech Out agrees — that technology must return to its place as a supplemental tool, not the center of education.

Read at nytimes.com →
NYT · Investigation

"Chromebook Remorse" — Natasha Singer

Singer's March 29, 2026 investigation is the piece that pushed this conversation from grassroots to mainstream. She visited McPherson, Kansas, where middle-school principal Inge Esping had pulled 480 Chromebooks out of circulation after watching students use them to watch YouTube, play games, and bully classmates. The story has since catalyzed legislation in sixteen states — including Minnesota.

Read at nytimes.com →
Book · Jared Cooney Horvath

The Digital Delusion (2025)

An educational neuroscientist's book-length argument that edtech has consistently fallen short of its early promises. Horvath synthesizes the research: reading comprehension collapses on screens, attention spans shrivel, skills learned in gamified apps do not transfer when the screen is off. His Senate testimony has been viewed more than two million times. It's a useful starting point for any Duluth parent, teacher, or Board member trying to think through the trade-offs.

Find the book →
BBC · News Feature

Why Swedish Schools Are Bringing Back Books

Sweden rolled tablets into every classroom in the 2010s. Then fourth-grade reading comprehension scores fell sharply between 2016 and 2021. The government allocated roughly $60 million to buy physical textbooks, another $54 million for fiction and non-fiction, and launched a campaign whose slogan — från skärm till pärm, "from screen to binder" — says it all.

Read at bbc.com →
PLOS ONE · Peer-Reviewed

Screen Time & Adolescent Outcomes (ABCD Study, n=11,875)

The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study — one of the largest longitudinal studies of American children — found that higher screen time in 9- and 10-year-olds was associated with worse mental health, lower academic performance, more behavioral problems, and poorer sleep. Not definitive proof of causation, but a clear, consistent warning signal.

Read on PubMed Central →
OECD · Education Report

Digital Devices & Math Performance (OECD, 2026)

The OECD's January 2026 review of Swedish education found a correlation between extensive use of digital devices in math lessons and lower academic results. Andreas Schleicher, the OECD's education director, has publicly warned other countries against Sweden's "extreme" early adoption of technology in schools.

OECD education research →
Frontiers in Public Health · Review

Nature-Specific Outdoor Learning: A Systematic Review

A synthesis of 147 studies found that learning outdoors in nature produced increased student engagement, measurable academic improvement, stronger social and collaborative skills, and better self-regulation. Denmark's udeskole ("outdoor school") program — weekly outdoor lessons regardless of weather — shows improvements in reading performance, prosocial behavior, and motivation.

Book · Jonathan Haidt

The Anxious Generation

The social psychologist's 2024 book argues that a generation of children was moved onto screens without adequate evidence of benefit and with growing evidence of serious harm. Haidt has publicly pointed to Sweden's textbook reversal as a model of evidence-based policymaking. The book has become a touchstone for parents and school boards nationwide.

§ 05 — Duluth Already Leads

We do this for four-year-olds. Let's do it for fourteen-year-olds.

Duluth is not starting from zero on outdoor education. Duluth is, quietly, one of the national centers for it. It's worth noticing that while our public school district uses Chromebooks in every elementary classroom, the university across the hillside has been training the country's outdoor educators for half a century.

The University of Minnesota Duluth's Bachelor of Applied Science in Environmental and Outdoor Education is one of the longest-standing programs of its kind in the United States. The university added a Childhood Nature Studies major in 2021, led by Prof. Julie Ernst, expressly designed to train educators in nature-based learning for young children.

Parents of four- and five-year-olds in this city have been choosing this model in increasing numbers, because it works. Across 147 studies, outdoor and nature-based learning produces higher engagement, better academic outcomes, stronger social skills, and — critically — fewer symptoms in children with ADHD and other attention challenges. This is exactly the population affected most by heavy Chromebook use.

So here is the Take Tech Out position, in one sentence: Duluth is already a national leader in getting young children outside. The opportunity in front of us is to extend that leadership up through elementary and middle school — the years when the mental health challenges facing American kids are peaking, and the years when getting them off screens and into the world around them would help most.
